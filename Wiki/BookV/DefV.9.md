# Definition V.9

## Statement

When three magnitudes are proportional, the first is said to have to the third the *duplicate ratio* of that which it has to the second.

## Guide

*Note: in D. Joyce's edition the Guide is shared with Definition V.8, Definition V.9, Definition V.10.*

In the illustration *A, B,* and *C* form three terms for the proportion *A* : *B* = *B* : *C,* therefore the ratio *A* : *C* is the duplicate ratio of *A* : *B.* For a numerical example, 4:9 is the duplicate ratio of 2:3. The illustration also shows a continued proportion of four magnitudes, *A, B,* *C,* and *D,* since *A* : *B* = *B* : *C* = *C* : *D.* Also, *A* : *D* is the triplicate ratio of *A* : *B.* For a numerical example, 8 : 27 is the triplicate ratio of 2 : 3. | ![java applet or image](defV8.gif)

Continued proportions, also known as geometric progressions

In the second book on number theory, Book VIII, Euclid considers continued numeric proportions of arbitrary length such as

64, 96, 144, 316, 324, 486

See, for example, [VIII.1](../BookVIII/PropVIII.1.md). These are commonly called *geometric progressions* or *geometric sequences*. In a geometric progression, the ratio of each term to the next term is the same. Euclid finds the sum of numerical geometric progression in [IX.35](../BookIX/PropIX.35.md).
